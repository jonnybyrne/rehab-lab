Essay 02 · Animated epidemiology

The typical spinal cord injury patient is 15 years older than in 1973.

By a PM&R resident physician

Why this essay: Spinal cord injury is the heart of physiatry, and its demographics shifted while nobody was looking.

Surviving the injury was only the first change. The patients got older, the causes tilted toward falls, and the rehab system built around the young trauma patient has not caught up.

The hook

The patient moved fifteen years to the right.

You might be thinking that number sounds high for this injury. Across nine year-of-injury cohorts, mean age at injury rose to 43.2, and “typical” in this essay always means that mean, not the median or the most common case. A newer data-sheet window, “since 2015” through August 2025, puts the current anchor at 44.3. That anchor is related, but it is not a tenth point in the older cohort series.

The clinical version: a rehab system built around the young trauma patient increasingly meets a middle-aged or older adult, often after a fall.

A sampled historyThe National SCI Database follows patients treated at SCI Model Systems centers. It is not a census of every U.S. injury.

Etiology crossover

Falls nearly caught car crashes.

Falls kept climbing while crashes kept sliding. Crashes still lead, but a 30.4-point gap narrowed to just 4.6 points.

You would expect the aging to show up everywhere, and it nearly does: Chen and colleagues3 found it across sex, race, and every cause except violence. People injured through violence stayed young.

Where survival stalled

The first year improved. The years after it stalled.

Since the 1970s, first-year life expectancy for people with tSCI improved steadily. For those who survived that first year, the published overall life-expectancy estimates have not budged since the early 1980s, and they still sit below the general population. An unchanged overall average does not prove that every subgroup gained nothing; the source publishes no subgroup trend that would settle the question.

Methodology & sources

What the database can and cannot say

  • Model Systems sample: these era trends describe patients treated at federally funded SCI Model Systems centers, not a complete national registry. The NSCISC database page currently estimates capture at roughly 6% of new SCI cases in the United States. Scene deaths are excluded.
  • Separate national estimates: incidence comes from Jain et al. 2015; prevalence from Lasfargues et al. 1995. They are not counts from the Model Systems database.
  • Cohort definitions changed over time: 1972-79 through 2015-21 cohorts come from the 2021 Annual Statistical Report. "Since 2015" anchors use a still-growing window through August 2025 and are shown separately.
  • Complication categories changed: medical/surgical complications were separated from "Other" in later reporting.
  • Life expectancy: period estimates from the 2026 Data Sheet, awaiting expert cross-check.
  • Mean only: the dashboard plots each era's published mean age as a single dot on the age axis. The source table publishes no age distribution for these cohorts, so no histogram is drawn.
  • Chart data: era anchors and cause shares are in data/sci-cohort/.
