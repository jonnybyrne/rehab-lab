The Spring That Has to Arrive on Time

A brace for the ankle looks like a support, and support is part of the job: an ankle foot orthosis, or AFO, can hold the foot up, position it for swing, and control how it meets the ground. A spring-like AFO also does work in time. Follow one ideal passive spring: it bends while the foot is on the ground, stores energy the body put into it, and returns part of that energy at push-off, just before the foot leaves the ground. The return adds no new muscle energy, and some stored energy is lost. If the return lands at the right moment, walking can cost less. If it lands at the wrong moment, or if there was nothing for it to replace, the same brace can do nothing at all.

By a PM&R resident physician

Start in the gait lab. A patient walks a runway over force plates that record the force between foot and ground. Small retroreflective markers sit on the hip, the knee, and the ankle, and cameras track them through each step. The lab can measure two things a clinic room cannot: how stiff the brace itself is, in a bench test, and what walking costs the person wearing it. Walking energy cost here means the energy needed to cover a distance under the test conditions of a study.

Step 3 of 4 · Terminal stance · Fully loaded

At the end of stance the spring is at its deepest bend and the force arrow has swung forward past the ankle. The energy is all borrowed, held for a fraction of a second. What happens next decides whether the borrowing helped.

The heel lifts, the spring straightens, and the push-off arrow flares as the stored energy returns into the step. The brace borrowed energy early and repaid it late, and whether that trade helps depends on when the repayment lands.

Scene four · Timing beats storage

Same energy, different moment

One question is left. If a patient does have the deficit, what setting helps most in the model? The instinctive answer is more: a stiffer spring that stores more energy should help more. Bregman 2011 tested that instinct in a simplified two dimensional model with a passive spring at each ankle and constant torques at the hips. It was not a clinical fitting trial. The instinct failed. An optimal stiffness existed in the model, and maximum energy storage was not the most efficient setting. With maximum storage, push-off came too late to reduce the impact of the other leg landing. What mattered was when the energy came back, relative to the step on the other side.

Scene five · The prescription

What a carbon spring did in ten patients

So what happened when a spring-like carbon AFO was measured in patients? In Bregman 2012, titled Spring-like Ankle Foot Orthoses reduce the energy cost of walking by taking over ankle work, ten patients with stroke or multiple sclerosis walked with and without the AFO. It accounted for 60 percent of positive ankle work, and walking energy cost fell by 9.8 percent. The saving did not come from a bigger net push-off: net ankle push-off was not augmented. The AFO took over part of the ankle work, so the leg itself did less. Ten patients show what this device did in that study, not a maximum and not an optimized prescription.

That result has conditions. For 3 of 7 patients in the 2010 cohort, no reduction in walking energy cost was measured. The stiffness needs to sit near an optimum in the model, not at a maximum, because the efficient pattern is a timed return just before the other foot strikes. And energy cost is only one outcome in a real fitting.

The clinician and orthotist match more than a spring: patient goals, swing clearance, stance and knee control, comfort, and footwear, with functional reassessment after fitting. A brace that saves energy but trips, hurts, or will not fit the shoe has not solved the problem.

The Map That Moves

The surface of the brain holds a map of the body. One strip of cortex receives touch from the skin, and each part of the body owns a territory on it. The lips and fingers own wide territories; the trunk, a narrow one. When a limb is lost, its territory does not simply stay empty. In 1995, in a selected sample of 13 arm amputees, the cortical source for face touch sat shifted toward the former hand territory, and that shift rose with how much the missing hand hurt, at a correlation of r equal to 0.93. That r is a strong association across those 13 people, not a percentage of pain explained. Later work would challenge the shift as the mechanism.

By a PM&R resident physician

Phantom limb pain is pain felt in a limb that is no longer there. It is common. In a national survey of 914 people with limb loss, interviewed by telephone after contacting the Amputee Coalition of America between 1998 and 2000, 79.9 percent reported phantom pain in the previous four weeks. For decades that was hard to explain. This essay follows one answer from cartography: in people who had lost an arm, the measured position of one stretch of coastline differed between groups, an answer later evidence would complicate.

Scroll · Survey, compare, measure, question

Scene one · The flat map

A coastline of touch

Start with the map at rest. The somatosensory strip runs across the brain from the side toward the top, and the body is laid out along it in a fixed order. These charts show a simplified upper body portion of that strip, for one hemisphere. The leg and foot territories sit outside them. At the lower, outer end sits the face, with the lips at the very edge. Then come the fingers and the hand, then the arm, then the trunk. Every chart of this strip, called a homunculus, shows the same proportions. Territories are sized by use and sensitivity, not by body size. Each territory below is the patch of cortex that answers when that body part is touched.

Two features matter for everything that follows. First, the territories touch: the face sits directly beside the hand, with no empty ground between them. Second, the map is maintained by input. Every touch from the hand is a signal that keeps its territory claimed, and when the signals stop, the map can change. The face is the hand's nearest neighbor, and borders here are habits, not walls.

Scene two · The neighbors

Why the face is the hand's neighbor

A flat map already carries the one fact this essay needs, with no second scale. In the classic homunculus drawing, representation is disproportionate: the lips, the face, and the fingers occupy wide territories relative to their size on the body, while the trunk occupies a narrow one. That is a qualitative feature of the drawing, not a set of measured heights. What matters here is adjacency.

Read the strip in order and the face territory ends exactly where the fingers and hand begin. No gap, no buffer, no other territory in between. So when researchers measured where face touch was represented after an arm was lost, the comparison that mattered was between two neighbors: the face source on the amputation side, against its mirror image on the intact side.

Scene five · A later challenge

The hand that would not leave

In 2013, Makin and colleagues studied 18 people with arm amputation and looked at the former hand territory itself. They found the opposite emphasis: stronger phantom pain went with a more preserved former hand representation, in activity and in structure, and lip movements showed no increased activity in the former hand area and no meaningful link to pain. On that account, pain tracks a hand representation that refuses to fade.

Phantom pain involves peripheral processes as well as central ones, as Flor and colleagues noted in 1995. Their own data drew the lines: stump pain and nonpainful phantom sensations, measured separately, did not track the cortical shift. Phantom pain is pain in the missing limb. Residual limb pain is pain in the limb that remains. A nonpainful phantom sensation is awareness of the missing limb without pain. None of this makes the pain less real. The atlas is a clue, not a verdict.

Scene six · Why rehab cares

Border diplomacy

Mirror therapy places a mirror between the arms so the reflection of the intact hand appears where the missing hand should be. What returns is visual feedback. No touch signal travels from the missing hand, because there is no hand to send one. Sensory discrimination training is different: it delivers real stimulation to the residual limb and practices telling one stimulus from another. Neither literally hands territory back, and neither is a proven way to move a border home.

The evidence is genuinely mixed. A small trial of sensory discrimination training reported reductions in phantom pain and in cortical reorganization against a medically treated control group (Flor et al. 2001). For mirror therapy, a 2023 systematic review restricted to randomized placebo controlled trials found five trials, only one with a significant benefit, and could not conclude that mirror therapy reduces phantom pain (Guémann et al. 2023). The honest summary: early and mixed evidence, some patients helped, no promises. What Flor 1995 changed permanently is narrower than a cure: a measurable difference that tracked pain in one small sample, and a hypothesis with coordinates to test and refine.

Essay 39 · Multiple Sclerosis · What If

What If a Hot Shower Could Take Your Legs Away for an Hour?

A hot shower can do it. Raise core temperature by half a degree Celsius, a change too small to feel as fever, and a demyelinated nerve fiber starts dropping signals: vision smears, legs go heavy, fatigue floods in. Cool down and the signals come back. Nothing new has been damaged. The wire was never cut. It was only warm.

By a PM&R resident physicianOne day, one axon, scrubbed by scroll

First · The observation

A sign found in 1890, in the eye

In 1890 the ophthalmologist Wilhelm Uhthoff described patients with optic neuritis whose vision faded when they exercised and returned when they rested. The sign still carries his name, and it was visual from the start: heat picked out the optic nerve first, and showed what warmth does to a nerve stripped of its insulation.

It is not rare. In a Swedish survey, 256 people with multiple sclerosis answered questions about heat, and 58 percent reported sensitivity to it, tied most closely to fatigue. Their triggers are ordinary: a shower, a summer sidewalk, a fever, a workout. The body warms by fractions of a degree, and function they had at breakfast is gone by the time they are dressed.

Second · The mechanism

Why half a degree is enough

A healthy axon conducts with margin to spare. Myelin insulates each segment, the signal renews itself at the bare nodes between segments, and the safety factor is generous. Demyelination spends that margin. In 1971 Davis and Jacobson showed that injured and demyelinated nerve is exquisitely sensitive to temperature, and proposed it as the model for heat effects in multiple sclerosis.

Warmth shortens each electrical pulse and keeps the sodium channels that rebuild it switched off for longer. A myelinated fiber absorbs that cost. A bare segment, conducting at the edge, cannot: the pulse arrives too weak to trigger the next node, and conduction blocks. The more myelin is missing, the smaller the rise that does it. The body's own cooling is blunted too: in one study of 18 patients against 18 controls, the sweat response lagged as core temperature climbed.

Third · The proof, and its price

The hot bath test

Doctors once proved it the direct way: immerse the patient in hot water and watch. In 1981 Malhotra and Goren reported 20 patients: 17 worsened, and 12 developed 34 new signs between them. Two years later Berger and Sheremata reported four patients left considerably debilitated for a prolonged period, and urged caution. By the mid 1990s the test had been retired, replaced by safer and more specific studies.

This site has met the opposite trick. In Speed Trap, cold slows a healthy nerve and fools a conduction study into reporting damage that is not there. Heat in multiple sclerosis is the mirror image: the damage is real, the wire is bare, and a fraction of a degree is enough to silence it, honestly and reversibly, until the temperature falls.

Fourth · The rehab ledger

Cooling is a treatment. So is training.

If warmth is the problem, cooling is treatment, and it has been trialed. In a sham controlled crossover study of 84 heat sensitive patients run with NASA, garment cooling produced a small but measurable improvement in a composite of function, and patients reported less fatigue across a month of daily use. The authors called the effect modest. Modest and real is still a tool.

Before exercise, cooling buys headroom. In a study of six thermosensitive patients, half an hour with the legs in water at 16 to 17 degrees Celsius kept temperature, heart rate, and effort lower through half an hour of cycling, and the walk test and fatigue scores that collapsed without precooling held up with it. In a pool, the National Multiple Sclerosis Society sets its exercise classes at 80 to 84 degrees Fahrenheit, about 27 to 29 degrees, because warmer water fatigues the very people in the class.

The deepest change is not a garment. For many years, as Petajan and White wrote in 1999, patients with multiple sclerosis were advised to avoid exercise, partly from fear of exactly this warming. Then Petajan's group trained 54 patients for 15 weeks, three 40 minute sessions a week, and fitness and strength rose significantly while fatigue eased. Exercise raises core temperature, and it still helps. The modern answer is not avoidance. It is timing, precooling, cooler water, and rest built into the plan.

Uhthoff's phenomenon is called a pseudoexacerbation, a false alarm with real symptoms. It frightens people because it feels like a relapse. The difference matters: a relapse is new inflammation and new damage. This is old damage, briefly audible when the wire runs warm, and quiet again when it cools.

What If Half Your World Disappeared?

By a PM&R resident physician

Imagine eating breakfast and clearing only the right half of the plate, then pushing it away, certain you are full. Your eyes work. The food on the left never reached the mind's list of what counts. That is hemispatial neglect, a disorder of attention after stroke, and this essay walks its length: the room as it is, the room as it becomes, and the work of bringing it back.

Station 1 of 9 · The room, whole

Everything on the table counts

Start with the room as it should be: plate full on both sides, clock with twelve numbers, door left, window right. Attention is the system keeping it all in play.

The baseline. Vision and touch test normal while attention fails. Neglect is diagnosed by what someone does with a whole world.

Station 2 of 9 · The plate

Breakfast, half finished forever

After a right hemisphere stroke, the left side of the plate stops registering. The food is not hidden and the eyes are not broken. It is unattended: for the person eating, it does not exist.

How common. In one acute cohort of 750 people, neglect appeared in 43 percent of right hemisphere strokes and 20 percent of left. Ringman et al., 2004.

Station 3 of 9 · The clock

Half a clock, drawn with confidence

Ask for a clock and the numbers crowd onto the right. Many patients do not know anything is missing: the missing half is missing from awareness too, which is why arguing rarely helps.

The test and its shadow. Paper tests expose the gap. Unawareness of it has a name, anosognosia, and daily life scales rate it by asking patients to judge the same tasks a clinician scores.

Station 4 of 9 · The line and the stars

The middle of the line moves right

Given a line, the centre mark lands right. Given a page of stars, the left ones stay uncrossed. Single tests miss people; the battery, and watching real life, catches more.

How it is caught. Among 206 people after right hemisphere stroke, about 85 percent showed some neglect on at least one measure, and watching daily life was the most sensitive measure of all. Azouvi et al., 2002.

Station 5 of 9 · The page

Reading that starts in the middle

Reading slides right: the first words of each line vanish, sentences begin mid thought, and the reader blames the book. The same pull bends writing, dressing and shaving toward the right.

Not an eye problem. The letters on the left can be visible when pointed out, one by one. What fails is the pull of attention that starts every line at its beginning.

Station 6 of 9 · The corridor

The wheelchair finds every left doorframe

Out in the corridor the deficit stops being a test score. The chair drifts right and clips the left side of every doorway. Grooming, dressing and collisions are where the daily damage happens.

Measured in real life. The Catherine Bergego Scale scores 10 tasks, from grooming to wheelchair driving, and caught neglect that paper tests missed; moderate to severe behavioural neglect appeared in 36 percent of that cohort. Azouvi et al., 2003; Azouvi et al., 2002.

Station 7 of 9 · Rehab, the anchor

Teaching the eyes to start at the left

Scanning training fights the pull directly: a bold anchor at the left edge, a deliberate sweep, until starting at the left becomes the habit. Moving the left limb in left space can add to it.

What guidelines say. The evidence reviews rate visuospatial rehabilitation including visual scanning training as a practice standard for left neglect after right hemisphere stroke, their strongest grade. Cicerone et al., 2019.

Station 8 of 9 · Rehab, the prisms

Bend the world, and the brain bends back

Prism glasses shift everything seen ten degrees right. Reaching under them recalibrates the brain leftward, and in the first study the correction outlasted the glasses. The drawing is a labelled model, not a measurement.

The first result. After 50 pointings through prisms shifting vision 10 degrees right, pointing moved from about 9 degrees right of straight ahead to about 2 degrees right, and gains lasted at least 2 hours. Rossetti et al., 1998.

Station 9 of 9 · The room, again

The left side, back on effort

Time and practice bring much of the left back, rarely all of it and rarely for free. The room is whole again, but it must be searched on purpose, by someone who knows the left can go missing.

The honest limits. By three months, neglect persisted in 17 percent of right and 5 percent of left hemisphere strokes in that cohort, and it marked slower, smaller recovery across a year. Ringman et al., 2004; Katz et al., 1999.

What If You Stayed in Bed for Three Weeks?

In 1966, five healthy 20 year olds in Dallas volunteered to do nothing: three weeks of complete bed rest, no weight bearing, the way heart attacks were treated at the time. It cost them more than being alive did. Their maximal oxygen uptake fell 27 percent in twenty days. Thirty years of aging, measured in the same five bodies, took only about 12 percent. Then the story turns: at about age 50, six months of training handed the whole thirty years back. This essay races the two clocks on one gauge, then asks what the race means for a hospital ward that still prescribes the bed.

The rule of the what if series is simple. The counterfactual is the hook, never the evidence. Every measured number below carries its source, the tank's path between endpoints is drawn rather than measured and labeled as such, and nothing here is a prediction for any one patient. What makes this case special is that the experiment was actually run, and then life itself ran the control arm for thirty years.

Scene four · The prescription

The ward today

It is worth pausing on why five healthy men were in bed at all. In 1966, strict bed rest was not a research cruelty; it was the treatment. A heart attack earned weeks of it, enforced. The Dallas protocol simply copied the prescription into healthy volunteers and measured its price in the only bodies that could safely pay it.

The price changed practice. In their fifty year account, the Dallas authors write that the results showed strict bed rest to be harmful for heart attack patients and moved care, almost immediately, toward early ambulation and cardiac rehabilitation. The prescription had been part of the disease, and for once the measurement arrived in time to retire it.

And yet the ward day survived the lesson. In 2009, researchers put accelerometers on 45 hospitalized veterans aged 65 and older, most of them able to walk independently, and simply recorded the day. Nobody ordered those men to stay in bed. The bed is just where a hospital day happens, unless somebody designs it otherwise.

Bed rest also turned out too useful to retire. It remains the standard Earth bound analog for spaceflight, which is why the seventeen week bone study in the cascade exists: astronauts cannot be unloaded for science, so bedded volunteers stand in. The bed is a laboratory instrument now, and a hospital default. It stopped being a treatment.

Scene five · The reversal

Rehab is the training clock, run on purpose

The reversal is the reason this story belongs in a rehabilitation journal's neighborhood. In 1966 the refill took eight weeks of intensive training and overshot: 3.9 liters per minute, 18 percent above the men's own baseline. The young cardiovascular system, given work, relearns fast and then some.

At 50 the refill was slower, and the ledger had changed sides. The men came back on average 25 percent heavier, body fat doubled from 14 to 28 percent, fat free mass nearly intact. Their maximal cardiac output was undiminished; the entire thirty year decline ran through oxygen extraction, the muscle side of the ledger. Six months of training reversed it through that same side: all of it, back to 3.3, though none of the five reached the 3.9 of their youth.

There is a coda. Ten more years, one more measurement: at 60, the tank read 2.4 again, in men now carrying hypertension and atrial fibrillation. Bed rest at 20 had left the tank exactly where forty years of aging did, and the decline had accelerated, from about 13 milliliters a year in the first three decades to about 50 a year in the last one.

That is the PM&R reading, and its boundary matters as much as its headline. Five young healthy men are a demonstration, not a dosage: their numbers are 1966 group means, and no patient's plan should be read off their tank. What transfers is the shape of the thing. Capacity drains in days, in a known order, and refills in months of training, which is why getting a patient vertical early is not kindness. It is the treatment the Dallas five accidentally prescribed.

Recap

So what have we learned

Twenty days in bed cost five young men 27 percent of their aerobic capacity; thirty years of aging cost about 12; six months of training at 50 returned them in full. Underneath: stroke volume collapsed within weeks, muscle and insulin sensitivity gone in one week, heel bone down a tenth in seventeen weeks, hip bone still down half a year later. At 60 the tank read 2.4 again, the bed rest value from age 20. The two clocks are not close, and the fast one is the one medicine used to order.

The PM&R Files · Essay 34 · The first what if

What If You Ran a Marathon on the Moon?

Gravity writes the rules of walking and running, and on the Moon the rules invert. Lunar gravity is about one sixth of Earth's, 0.17 g. Down there, in reduced gravity experiments and in the Apollo film archive, running turns cheaper than walking, the walk to run switch trips earlier, and the body invents a third gait. Project the measured costs forward and the clock halves, on paper. Then the wall arrives: air, heat, and a self carrying suit. Nobody has run a marathon on the Moon. Here is what the numbers say would happen if somebody tried.

By a PM&R resident physician

First, the rule of this series: the counterfactual is the hook, never the evidence. Every measured number carries its source. Anything drawn between measurements is dashed and labeled model drawn; anything projected is labeled a model, assumptions printed beside it. The Moon is the ideal first case: twelve astronauts moved around up there in pressurized suits, and Earth laboratories have been unloading runners ever since.

Scene one · The course

A dashed line on a dark map

Set the course first. A marathon is 42.195 kilometers. On the Moon there is no road, no breathable atmosphere, and no guaranteed shade, so picture the dashed line below, lander to crater rim. Crews worked inside a shrinking circle set by suit supplies; Apollo 17's longest excursion ranged 7.6 kilometers from the Lunar Module, by rover.

The longest excursion of the Apollo program lasted 7 hours and 37 minutes. Apollo 17 drove its rover 35.7 kilometers across three excursions: less than a marathon. And the locomotion was improvised: of 38 gait events classified from film and reports, only 10 were walking; the rest were loping and running.

Scene two · The gravity machine

Turn gravity down and the gaits trade places

On Earth, walking is the cheap gait and it is not close. A walking mile uses about half the energy of a running mile. Walking has a best speed, a gentle one, where its cost bottoms out near 2 joules per kilogram per meter. Running costs about 4, roughly the same at any speed. So Earth runners walk to the start line and run the race.

Gravity is the load both gaits carry, taxed unequally. Farley and McMahon hung runners in harnesses that lifted away part of their weight. Cut gravity to a quarter: running's energy rate fell 72 percent, nearly in proportion to the weight removed; walking's fell only 33 percent. Running spends its energy supporting body weight against gravity, step after step; walking splits its bill between support, leg swing, and trading height for speed.

Step 1 of 4 · Earth, 1.00 g

Start where the reader lives. The walking curve dips to its minimum near 2 joules per kilogram per meter, the running line rides flat at about 4, and the switch trips at 2.16 meters per second, Froude number about 0.5. On Earth, walking wins every slow meter.

Step 2 of 4 · Half gravity, 0.50 g

Half gravity is the neighborhood of the flip. The transition Froude number, from the published fit, rises to 0.56, and the switch falls to 1.62 meters per second. Both curves here are model drawn between measured anchors: solid anchors, drawn path.

Step 3 of 4 · Mars, 0.38 g

Mars, at 0.38 g, sits below the inversion threshold. The fit puts the transition Froude number at 0.63 and the switch at a derived 1.49 meters per second. The Froude number rises even as the speed falls, because the scale it is measured against shrinks faster.

Step 4 of 4 · The Moon, 0.17 g

At one sixth gravity, the inversion completes. The unsuited switch falls to 1.18 meters per second, and an Apollo suit dropped it to about 0.75, at Froude 0.36, fitted. Running now costs about 2 joules per kilogram per meter, Earth walking's price, and in the conditions studied it costs less per meter than walking.

Scene three · What Apollo actually did

The film archive votes for the bounce

Carr and McGee classified every gait event they could measure in the Apollo Lunar Surface Journal and NASA reports: 38 in all. Ten were walking. Ten were loping, the bounding gait they settled into. Eighteen were running. The mean Froude number across all 38 was 0.49. The authors work one example: Neil Armstrong, running across the television picture on Apollo 11, about 22 meters in about 25 seconds, 0.89 meters per second. Nine of the events come from a single three minute gait evaluation by Aldrin, so the counts show what could be classified, not how crews usually traveled.

Then the suit rewrote the numbers: a pressurized suit is a balloon shaped like a person, and its pressure, 26.2 kilopascals, pushes its joints toward straight. Carr and McGee's idealized calculation has this force beating the lunar weight of the heaviest suit, the 96 kilogram A7LB, by a factor of 2.8: on the Moon, the suit carries itself. That, on their main comparison, is the paper's explanation for 60 percent of why the suited switch tripped at Froude 0.36 instead of 0.90. It is also why Charles Duke kept jumping on Apollo 16 to bend his knee and retrieve a dropped hammer.

Scene five · The wall

Why nobody has run it

So why has nobody done it? The marathon's opponent on the Moon is not gravity. It is the life support budget. Apollo's rule was the walkback limit: never roam farther than your remaining oxygen, carbon dioxide scrubbing, and cooling water let you walk home. The safe circle shrinks every hour. Heat is the sharper edge of the same budget: two Apollo 16 traverses were estimated at 326 and 429 watts, a heat load the backpack must shed.

Add the ground. The surface is dust over rubble, and how a boot meets that loose soil could itself decide the gait, the treadmill researchers suggested. Above everything sits the clock: 7 hours and 37 minutes is the longest anyone has worked outside on the Moon, wearing up to 96 kilograms of suit. A marathon at model pace fits inside that clock; at Apollo paces, nowhere close. Redraw the course as loops past the lander, each banking enough supply to walk home, and the design problem stops being the distance.

What would settle it is not a better model but a tested suited pace, a tested route, and a support plan. The gap between those two statements is the essay.

Scene six · The rehab bridge

The same dial hangs in the clinic

The coda is why this essay lives in a rehabilitation journal's neighborhood. The rigs that simulated lunar gravity are cousins of the body weight support treadmills in rehab gyms; Carr and McGee note the kinship. Unload a patient after spinal cord injury and the same question appears: how much support changes which gait is cheapest, and where the transition sits. In the gym the dials turn separately: harness support up, treadmill speed up, while the therapist watches step quality, effort, and tolerance. A harness changes the load the patient bears; gravity still acts on the swinging limbs.

Carr and McGee worked the exoskeleton example: a frame carrying a second body weight's worth of load, a prediction that the switch falls from about 2.2 meters per second to about 1.5. That is an added load path, not a harness unloading a patient, and not a prescription. What carries over is narrower: support changes the task's demands, and the response depends on the device and the person. The lope has a laboratory cousin, too: at lunar gravity, skipping costs almost exactly what running costs, a third gait in the repertoire.

Recap

So what have we learned

Gravity is not a backdrop to gait; it is the pricing mechanism. On Earth walking is cheap and running dear. Turn gravity down and running's price falls in proportion to weight while walking's barely moves, until below about 0.5 g the order inverts. The archive shows it: 10 walks, 10 lopes, 18 runs, in self carrying suits that reset the switch to Froude 0.36. Run the measured costs forward and the clock halves, if the power held. The walkback limit, the heat, and the record say nobody gets to try it yet.
