Two Shocks

Same injury, same word. Why are the two shocks opposites?

The headline number
In 490 patients with isolated spinal cord injury, neurogenic shock, defined as systolic blood pressure under 100 mmHg and heart rate under 80 beats per minute on arrival in the emergency department, appeared in 19.3 percent of cervical injuries (95 percent CI 14.8 to 23.7), 7 percent of thoracic injuries, and 3 percent of lumbar injuries (Guly, Bouamra and Lecky, Resuscitation 2008)

After a spinal cord injury, two different failures can share one name. Neurogenic shock is the hemodynamic one: the vessels lose their sympathetic squeeze, the pressure falls, and the pulse stays slow, because the heart's accelerator fibres are cut while the vagus brake, travelling outside the cord, stays connected. Spinal shock is the wiring one: reflexes and function below the injury switch off, then return in phases.

Counted strictly, the textbook picture is rarer than the teaching suggests. In the 490 patient cohort above, fewer than one in five cervical injuries showed the classical picture on arrival. In a second cohort of 84 patients followed for thirty days, the measured incidence swung with the definition used, reaching 29 percent of cervical injuries under the authors' own combined hemodynamic and laboratory criteria, with earlier reports gathered in that paper ranging from 14 to 44 percent (Ruiz et al., Journal of Neurotrauma 2018).

Spinal shock runs on the slower clock. In the four phase model of Ditunno and colleagues, day 0 to 1 is areflexia, days 1 to 3 bring initial reflex return, weeks 1 to 4 initial hyperreflexia, and months 1 to 12 final hyperreflexia and spasticity, as the cord below the injury, still alive, grows new synapses and turns its gain up. The phases are a model, not a measurement: the authors built them partly by extrapolating from basic neuroscience, and clinicians still disagree on what marks spinal shock's end.
