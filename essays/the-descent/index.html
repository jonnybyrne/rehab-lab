The Descent

Why does one coma fold its arms and another straighten them?

The headline number
In the IMPACT development set of 8,509 patients with moderate or severe traumatic brain injury, six month mortality was 28 percent and unfavourable outcome 48 percent, and the motor score was among the strongest predictors of outcome. The paper's own worked example, a 35 year old with motor score 3 (abnormal flexion) and both pupils reacting, carries core model risks of about 20 percent mortality and about 50 percent unfavourable outcome (Steyerberg et al., PLoS Medicine 2008)

After a severe brain injury, a patient who cannot follow commands still answers a painful stimulus with the arms. Some fold them across the chest; others straighten them stiffly. The two postures have names, decorticate and decerebrate, and generations of students have learned them as a level: where the damage sits relative to one small relay in the midbrain, the red nucleus.

The Glasgow Coma Scale turns the postures into numbers: abnormal flexion scores three, extension scores two. In 204,181 trauma records, the motor score alone carried nearly all of the full scale's predictive power, and the same total of four hides very different outlooks depending on how it is composed: predicted survival of 52, 73 or 81 percent (Healey et al., Journal of Trauma 2003).

The wiring picture comes with its failure modes attached. In humans the rubrospinal tract is small, and few of its fibres can be traced below the upper cervical segments (Nathan and Smith, Brain 1982), so the level story is a teaching model, not a localisation proof. Postures can be mixed or asymmetric, which is why the scale scores the best response, and no single feature predicts outcome on its own.
