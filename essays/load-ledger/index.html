The Load Ledger

What if your arms had to do your legs' job?

The headline number
Shoulder pain was reported in 30 to 71 percent of manual wheelchair users with traumatic spinal cord injury across 14 studies, and 58.5 percent of 130 respondents in the Dalyan cohort reported upper extremity pain (McCann et al., IntechOpen 2022; Dalyan et al., Spinal Cord 1999)

After spinal cord injury, mobility runs through the upper limbs: propulsion, transfers, and pressure relief all load the shoulder, a joint built for range rather than load-bearing. The essay keeps one ordinary day as a ledger, every push, transfer, and lift entered in turn and carried forward.

Multiplied across a decade, the drawn day becomes millions of strokes and tens of thousands of transfers and lifts, and the measured bill arrives as pain: shoulder pain in 30 to 71 percent across the studies in the McCann review, carpal tunnel syndrome reported in 49 to 73 percent, and in the Dalyan cohort pain that interfered with transfers in 65 percent of those doing them.

The day itself is drawn and labelled drawn on the page; the prevalence figures are measured and sourced. The preservation levers the Consortium guideline offers, the lightest fully customizable chair, long smooth strokes, better transfer setup, are printed as clinical practice directions, not measured effect sizes, and the essay says so plainly.

Sources: McCann A, Kerr D, Hannon-Fletcher MPA. In: Spinal Cord Injury and Rehabilitation (IntechOpen). 2022. Dalyan M, Cardenas DD, Gerard B. Spinal Cord. 1999;37(3):191 to 195. Paralyzed Veterans of America Consortium for Spinal Cord Medicine. Preservation of Upper Limb Function Following Spinal Cord Injury: A Clinical Practice Guideline for Health-Care Professionals. 2005.
