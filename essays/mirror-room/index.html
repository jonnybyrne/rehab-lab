The Mirror Room

Train one arm. Why does the other one get stronger?

The headline number
Across 31 randomized trials and 785 people, the side that never trained gained 11.9 percent in strength (95 percent confidence interval 9.1 to 14.8 percent); in a four week cast study, the casted wrist flexors lost 2.4 percent when the free arm trained, against 21.6 percent when both sides rested (Manca et al., European Journal of Applied Physiology 2017; Andrushko et al., Journal of Applied Physiology 2018)

One arm is in a cast. The other arm goes to the gym. That sounds like loyalty to the wrong limb, until the cast comes off and the arm that did nothing has kept its strength, while the arm in the identical cast, on the person who rested both sides, has lost more than a fifth of it.

The phenomenon has a name, cross-education, and it has been on the record since 1894: train one limb, and the same muscle on the other side gets stronger too, without moving, without growing. The strength arrives through the wiring, not the muscle. This page trains one side of a room and watches the other side's gauge.

When the casts came off, the trained group's casted wrist flexors had lost 2.4 percent of their strength. The resting group's had lost 21.6 percent. The casted muscle even held its size on the scan, and an earlier study with thirty volunteers in casts for three weeks found the same protection for strength. The protection is specific: it covered the trained flexor muscles, not their neighbours.

Journal of Applied Physiology. 2018;124(4):866 to 876. Magnus CRA, Arnold CM, Johnston G, Dal-Bello Haas V, Basran J, Krentz JR, Farthing JP.
