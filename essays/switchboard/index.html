The Switchboard

A stroke takes half a face. Why does the forehead keep working?

The headline number
Of 92 acute stroke patients with central facial palsy, 70 (76 percent) had some upper facial weakness when expressions were graded on video, usually milder than the lower face weakness; in a smaller carefully scored cohort, only 7 of 28 patients had weakness confined to the lower face (Hongdusit and Wongwandee, Neurology International 2025; Cattaneo et al., Annals of Neurology 2010)

A stroke weakens half the face, but the forehead on that side still wrinkles and the eye still closes. A palsy of the facial nerve itself takes the whole half, forehead included. Students memorize the pattern as a rule; this page draws the wiring that produces it, and then shows where the rule bends.

The facial nucleus has two halves with different managers. The lower face takes its orders from the motor strip of the opposite hemisphere, in middle cerebral artery territory. The forehead is supplied from motor areas on the inner wall of the hemisphere, in anterior cerebral artery territory, projecting to both sides. A common hemispheric stroke floods the first territory and spares the second, so the mouth droops and the forehead keeps working.

Graded on video, the sparing turns out to be a matter of degree: 76 percent of central facial palsies showed some upper face weakness, usually milder than the lower. And a stroke inside the pons, where the nerve fibres loop before they leave the brainstem, can take the whole face and counterfeit a nerve palsy, often with a gaze disturbance as the tell.
