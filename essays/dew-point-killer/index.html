Why does a humid 75°F morning feel so bad?

Why does humidity, not the temperature, decide how a run feels?

The headline number
Dew point breakpoints: humid above 65°F, oppressive above 75°F

A humid 75°F morning can feel worse than a dry 90°F afternoon. The dew point, not the temperature or the humidity percentage, decides how well your sweat can cool you.

The heat index folds air temperature and humidity into the load the body has to shed. It is defined from 80°F up.

The hotter day is obviously brutal. But the dew point catches something the thermometer and the heat index miss. The cooler morning leaves less room for your sweat to evaporate.

Relative humidity is actual vapor pressure divided by the saturation vapor pressure at the current temperature. Warm air holds far more water vapor, so the percentage shifts as the temperature shifts, even when the air gains no moisture. Dew point is the temperature at which that air would hit saturation: when the dew point climbs, the moisture content climbed with it. One humidity number cannot tell you how a run will feel: the same 70% relative humidity can describe a pleasant 55°F morning or a punishing 85°F one.

NOAA's 1991 to 2020 normals for Miami International Airport put the average dew point at 72.0°F in June, 73.0°F in July, 73.8°F in August, and 73.2°F in September. That is four straight months parked in the runner's "very humid" band.

Che Muhamed and colleagues randomized 11 trained male distance runners across five treadmill sessions at 31°C (87.8°F): 60 minutes at 70% VO₂max, then an incremental test to volitional exhaustion.

Sources: Ely et al. Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise. 2008. Che Muhamed et al. Temperature. 2016.
