What If You Had No Cerebellum at All?

The cerebellum holds 69 billion of the brain's 86 billion neurons on average (Azevedo et al. 2009) and helps calibrate movement. What happens when it never forms at all?

The headline number
Yu and colleagues (2015) tabulated nine living cases including this patient: eight had symptoms, and the one normal examination was a subtotal case

A 24 year old woman was found to have no cerebellum at all: fluid filled the posterior fossa where it should sit, and diffusion imaging showed no cerebellar connections. She had stood at 4, spoken intelligibly at 6, and walked at 7 (Yu et al. 2015).

The essay runs a watchmaker's calibration bench in three states, fitted, removed, and drawn as one possible compensation strategy, then walks her reported timeline and holds the authors' two conclusions together: early compensation made her deficits less than expected, and the cerebellum is still necessary for normal development.

A contrast with adult loss follows: in Schmahmann and Sherman's 20 patients with acquired cerebellar disease, a cognitive affective syndrome appeared that the congenital case largely spared. One case is a demonstration, not a prognosis.

Sources: Yu F, et al. Brain. 2015. Azevedo FAC, et al. 2009. Schmahmann JD, Sherman JC. Brain. 1998. Manto M, et al. 2012.
