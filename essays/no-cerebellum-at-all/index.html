What If You Had No Cerebellum at All?

The cerebellum holds 69 billion of the brain's 86 billion neurons on average (Azevedo et al. 2009) and helps calibrate movement. What happens when it never forms at all?

The headline number
Yu and colleagues (2015) tabulated nine living cases including this patient: eight had symptoms, and the one normal examination was a subtotal case

The cerebellum helps calibrate movement. Born without it, the prediction is no steady hand, clear speech, or balance. Yet the ninth living case tabulated by Yu and colleagues (Brain, 2015) had real, mild to moderate deficits: she walked and spoke, years late and unsteadily. Of nine cases tabulated including her, eight had symptoms; the one normal examination was subtotal.

The what if rule holds: the counterfactual is the hook, never the evidence. This is one published case (Yu and colleagues, Brain, 2015): complete primary cerebellar agenesis, not a rate, prognosis, or promise.

She was 24, admitted with dizziness and unsteady walking for over 20 years, plus nausea and vomiting for about a month. No cerebellar tissue was found. The posterior fossa, the space at the back of the skull, held cerebrospinal fluid, fluid that cushions the brain; the brainstem was slimmed, its pons (part of the brainstem) hypoplastic, smaller, not fully developed.

All three cerebellar artery types were missing on both sides: posterior inferior, anterior inferior, superior. Diffusion imaging showed no cerebellar connections. Conservative care relieved her presenting symptoms; relief held at a 4 year follow-up.

Azevedo and colleagues counted the cerebellum at about a tenth of brain mass in that sample, holding 69 billion of 86 billion neurons, against 16 billion in the cortex: rounded means from four adult male brains, estimating no individual's cells.

The cerebellum helps coordinate timing and accuracy, adjusting movement with practice: predicting consequences, comparing prediction with reality, using each error next time. The job card carries the consensus list.

Sources: Yu F, et al. Brain. 2015. Azevedo FAC, et al. 2009. Schmahmann JD, Sherman JC. Brain. 1998. Manto M, et al. 2012.
