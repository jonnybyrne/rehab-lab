The Map That Moves

After an arm is lost, the face moves into the hand territory on the brain map. Why does how far it moves track the pain?

The headline number
Cortical shift: 2.05 cm with phantom pain, 0.43 cm without; r = 0.93

The surface of the brain holds a map of the body, and each part of the body owns a territory on it. When a limb is lost, its territory does not simply stay empty.

In 1995, in 13 arm amputees, the cortical source for face touch sat shifted toward the former hand territory, and the size of the shift tracked the pain: 2.05 centimeters in those with phantom pain (n = 8) against 0.43 centimeters in those without (n = 5), a correlation of r = 0.93. A correlation is not a mechanism, and the essay says so.

In a national survey, 79.9 percent of 914 people with limb loss reported phantom pain in the previous four weeks. The essay follows the map, the challenge to it, and the therapies that aim at it.

Sources: Flor H, et al. Nature. 1995. Ephraim PL, et al. Archives of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation. 2005.
