What If a Hot Shower Could Take Your Legs Away for an Hour?

A rise of half a degree can silence a demyelinated nerve. Why does the signal come back when you cool down?

The headline number
In a Swedish survey, 58 percent of 256 people with multiple sclerosis reported sensitivity to heat (Flensner et al. 2011)

A small rise in core temperature slows and then blocks conduction in a demyelinated nerve: vision smears, legs go heavy, fatigue floods in. Cool down and the signal returns, because nothing new was damaged. It is a pseudoexacerbation, not a relapse.

The essay scrubs one day across one axon, from morning baseline through a hot shower, conduction block at the bare segment, cooling, and a training day: the rise is printed as a band, first tracked change at 0.2 degrees, about 0.5 most often named as sufficient, 0.8 the heated dose in the experiment.

Cooling is treatment, and so is training. Garment cooling gave a small, real functional gain in 84 patients; precooling preserved the walk in six; and a 15 week programme made 54 patients stronger, even though exercise raises temperature. For years these patients were told to avoid exercise. The modern answer is timing, precooling, cooler water, and rest built into the plan.

Sources: Flensner G, et al. BMC Neurology. 2011. Davis SL, et al. Neurology. 2008. Schwid SR, et al. Neurology. 2003. White AT, et al. Multiple Sclerosis. 2000. Petajan JH, et al. Annals of Neurology. 1996.
