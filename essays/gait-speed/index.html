How Fast Do You Walk?

What does your walking speed say about your health?

The headline number
Each 0.1 m/s faster: hazard ratio 0.88 for mortality (34,485 adults, 9 cohorts)

Temperature, pulse, breath, blood pressure, oxygen saturation: clinicians record five vital signs without thinking. In 2009, Fritz and Lusardi made the case for a sixth, the speed you walk at. It takes one hallway and one stopwatch, and it knows more about your future than most of the chart. Start by taking yours.

In 2011, Stephanie Studenski and colleagues pooled nine cohort studies: 34,485 community dwelling adults, all 65 or older, each timed on a short walk and then followed for years. 17,528 of them died during follow-up. One number sorted them with unsettling skill.

Predicted 10-year survival at age 75. For men aged 75, modeled survival ran from 19% at the slowest measured speeds to 87% at the fastest; for women aged 75, the same model ran 35% to 91%. Same birthday, same sex; the groups differ only in walking speed, and the link is an association, not a cause. The bars show the published range endpoints only, because the paper reports the range, not a curve to interpolate. These are pooled averages across thousands of walkers in the cohorts, not a forecast for the person holding the stopwatch.

The finding that made clinicians sit up came next: age, sex, and gait speed together predicted survival about as accurately as a fuller clinical model loaded with diagnoses and other measurements. A stopwatch and two facts matched the chart.

Clinics love batteries: multi item scores and long questionnaires that take twenty minutes and a trained examiner. In 2009, an International Academy on Nutrition and Aging task force (Abellan van Kan and colleagues) reviewed the evidence for this single hallway number and concluded that usual pace gait speed predicts the outcomes everyone actually cares about, at least as well as the composite tools, and that the four meter walk was the most used protocol in the literature.

Here is the part the other five vitals cannot offer. What makes the number worth training is that it is built out of trainable parts.

Sources: Studenski et al. JAMA. 2011. Fritz & Lusardi. Journal of Geriatric Physical Therapy. 2009.
