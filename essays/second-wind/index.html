The Second Wind

What if your muscles could not spend the sugar they store?

The headline number
The second wind arrived at 8 to 12 minutes, with oxygen uptake rising from 13.0 by more than 25 percent to 16.5; on 75 grams of sucrose, heart rate ran 34 beats per minute lower (Haller and Vissing 2002; Vissing and Haller, New England Journal of Medicine 2003)

In McArdle disease, one missing enzyme, myophosphorylase, locks the glycogen stored inside the muscle. The fuel tank is full, but the tap is chained shut, so the first minutes of exercise run on almost nothing and end in a forced stop.

Keep going gently and the picture flips: blood-borne fuel, glucose and fatty acids, reaches the muscle, and at 8 to 12 minutes the second wind arrives. Sugar taken before exercise borrows that delivery early, and fourteen weeks of moderate aerobic training raised work capacity by 36 percent in eight patients.

The evidence is small and the essay keeps it labelled: cohorts of six to twelve, no randomised trial of aerobic training at all, and the first minutes are genuinely dangerous, with rhabdomyolysis the trap for anyone who pushes through. Exercise advice here is supervised and individual, never a dose from a webpage.

Sources: Haller RG, Vissing J. Arch Neurol. 2002. Vissing J, Haller RG. N Engl J Med. 2003. Andersen ST, Haller RG, Vissing J. Arch Neurol. 2008. Haller RG, et al. Ann Neurol. 2006.
