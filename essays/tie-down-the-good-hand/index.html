Tie Down the Good Hand

After a stroke, the good hand does everything and the weak hand retires. What happens when a trial mittens the good hand for two weeks and makes the weak one work?

The headline number
Wolf Motor Function Test performance time fell from 19.3 to 9.3 seconds with constraint-induced movement therapy against 24.0 to 17.7 seconds under usual care, a between-group difference of 34 percent (Wolf et al., JAMA 2006)

EXCITE randomised 222 people, 3 to 9 months after a first stroke, across seven institutions. One group wore a mitt on the stronger hand during waking hours while practising shaped tasks with the weaker hand for two weeks; the other received usual care.

The practised hand got much faster on the laboratory timer, and the gains showed up in real life too: Motor Activity Log amount of use rose by 0.43 points more and quality of movement by 0.48 points more than under usual care, and the gains persisted to at least one year.

The door into the trial was narrow: only patients with some active wrist and finger extension could enter, so the result belongs to them. The control care was not standardised, and the mitt, the practice, and the team were randomised as one package, so the trial cannot say which ingredient did the work. The essay prints those limits on the page.

Sources: Wolf SL, et al. JAMA. 2006;296(17):2095 to 2104.
