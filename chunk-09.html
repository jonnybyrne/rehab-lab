The fastest marathon ever run was not a record.

In 2019, Eliud Kipchoge ran 1:59:40.2 in the Ineos run, with rotating pacers and lab-like conditions. It proved a human could break two hours. It was not a ratified race, so it never entered the record books.

Seven years later, the barrier fell for real. Sabastian Sawe ran 1:59:30 in London on April 26, 2026, in legal shoes, in a real race: ten seconds faster than the run that did not count.1

One treadmill test made running 4% cheaper.

Picture the setup: 18 elite men on a Colorado treadmill, running 14, 16, and 18 km/h in three mass-matched racing shoes. The Vaporfly prototype burned 4.16% less energy than the Nike Streak 6 and 4.01% less than the adidas Adios Boost 2. Both gaps cleared statistical muster (p<0.001).2

Hoogkamer et al. (2018) was funded by Nike, and two co-authors worked at the Nike Sport Research Lab; later independent analyses found the same direction of benefit.

Every runner in the study improved, which is striking. But look at who they were: fast men in their twenties, all wearing size 10, all on a treadmill. “Four percent” is a lab average, not a guarantee stamped on your receipt.

The street test pointed the same way.

The New York Times Upshot dug through thousands of paired Strava race records, including runners who raced the same events in different shoes. Runners in the new shoes ran 4 to 5% faster than in average shoes, and 1.5 to 2.5% faster than in the next-best shoe.4

It is observational: it cannot fully control training, weather, the course, who reports which shoe, or who even uses Strava. But it pointed the same direction as the lab.5

The shoe is not the whole story.

World Athletics stepped in in January 2020, effective April 30, with road-shoe limits: 40 mm maximum stack and no more than one rigid plate. That July it set track-spike limits at 20 mm for events up to 800 m and 25 mm above 800 m. Kipchoge’s Ineos shoe was reported at roughly 50 mm with multiple plates, which would fail today’s limits outright. Current models must also sit on retail shelves for at least four months, and approved shoes can be inspected during record ratification.7

The plate got the name, but the foam may do as much work. The original prototype changed foam, stack height, plate, and geometry all at once, so the plate’s solo contribution was never cleanly isolated; reviews suggest the foam may contribute as much as the plate, or more. The 2016-to-2026 change was a whole system: lower stack and conventional foam with no plate became 40 mm of resilient foam, one curved plate, and rocker geometry.8

Not everyone gets four percent. A September 2026 summary compared the top 50 road performances from 2009 to 2015 against 2017 to 2024, using throwing events as an era-wide control: women’s performances improved 4.51% against 2.62% for men, with throws near 1%. Possibly women’s records simply had more room to move; that is an interpretation, not a settled biological effect.9

The evidence on injury points both ways. Worth watching: lower cadence and more rearfoot eversion in advanced footwear; five case reports of navicular bone stress injuries in elite runners; surveys report ankle, Achilles, and plantar-fascia symptoms, though surveys cannot prove causation. Reassuring: lower ankle plantarflexion moments in the same 2026 PM&R study of 23 elite runners; a 2025 study found reduced muscle and joint forces; no increase in Achilles loading and no consistent plate effect on joint power or leg stiffness. No published study has measured actual injury rates in super-shoe versus conventional-shoe cohorts.10

The shoes are proven to make you faster. Nothing has proven they break you, or that they do not.

1:59:30

Legal shoes. A real race. The revolution won.

Sawe’s 1:59:30 needed no asterisks: legal shoes, a real race, and ten seconds faster than Kipchoge’s unofficial Ineos run.
