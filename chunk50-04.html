Early birds, late starters

The trial also answered the question every family asks: does waiting cost recovery? Ninth place · The Garden Gate

What LEAPS cannot say

Hold the tie at its true strength. LEAPS had no untreated arm: every participant received a structured, therapist-run programme, so the 52 percent who improved cannot be credited to the machine or to the home visits. People also recover on their own in the first year, and this design cannot separate therapy working from time passing. What the trial does rule out is superiority. The harness programme was not better than the home programme.

That is not proof that treadmill training does nothing. It is proof that the expensive version of walking practice was matched by the cheap version of everything else, and that the front door is a tougher opponent than the machine expected.

Tie Down the Good Hand

A stroke leaves one hand weak, so the other hand takes over, and the weak hand quietly retires. EXCITE tested the rudest fix in rehabilitation: put a mitt on the good hand so it cannot help, and make the weak hand do everything, intensively, for two weeks. In 222 people, three to nine months after a first stroke, the treated hand got faster in the laboratory and busier in real life, and the gains were still there a year later.

By a PM&R resident physicianThe Picture BookOne trial, one borrowed hand

Scene 1 · The problem

The hand that stopped asking

The problem has a name in the clinic: learned nonuse. In the first days after a stroke, the weak hand fumbles, drops, and fails, and every failure teaches the same lesson: stop asking it. The good hand buttons the shirt, lifts the cup, opens the door. The weak hand is carried, rested, and quietly excused from daily life. Practice is the only cure for that retirement, and practice is exactly what the good hand makes unnecessary. So the trial asked a blunt question: what happens if the good hand is not allowed to help?

The good hand does the day. The weak hand watches, and watching becomes the habit.

Scene 2 · The trial

A fair test of a blunt idea

EXCITE ran at seven academic institutions in the United States, between January 2001 and January 2003. It randomised 222 people, each three to nine months past a first stroke. One group, 106 people, drew constraint-induced movement therapy. The rest received usual and customary care, whatever their local services provided. Outcome assessors were blinded to assignment, the single blind a therapy trial can honestly run, because nobody can hide a mitt. The design asked two questions at once: can the hand move faster, and will the person actually use it?

The abstract prints the total, 222. The split, 106 to the mitt, is printed in the full text.

Scene 3 · The prescription

Two weeks of borrowed time

The prescription lasted two weeks. The less-affected hand, the good hand, went into a restraining mitt during waking hours, so it could not rescue a single task. The affected arm got the opposite treatment: intensive, repetitive task practice, shaped step by step: reaching, grasping, lifting, releasing, the ordinary movements of a day rebuilt as exercises. The control group simply carried on with usual care. Two weeks is a short sentence. The question the trial really asked was whether a fortnight of forced use could outlast itself.

One hand mittened, one hand put to work. That is the whole intervention, and the mitt stayed on during waking hours.

Scene 4 · The timer

The timer

In the laboratory, the Wolf Motor Function Test times a battery of tasks. At baseline the treatment group averaged 19.3 seconds; a year later it averaged 9.3 seconds, a fall of 52 percent. The control group improved too, from 24.0 to 17.7 seconds, a fall of 26 percent, because ordinary care is not nothing. The gap between the groups was 34 percent, with a 95 percent confidence interval of 12 to 51 percent, and a p value below 0.001.

The treatment lasted two weeks. The measurements ran for a year, and the gains were still present at twelve months: the faster times held, and the arm was still being used more, and better, in ordinary life. A fortnight of forced use, repaid over a year.

Scene 7 · The door

The door was narrow

Eligibility · Read before quoting the result

Read the result through the door it came in. To enrol, a patient had to have some active wrist and finger extension: the hand had to open, at least a little, before the mitt went on. That single condition decides who this result is about. EXCITE says nothing about the weakest arms, the ones with no voluntary extension at all, because nobody with such an arm was in the room. The answer belongs to the survivors who could already start.

The entry condition, drawn: a hand that can open, at least a little. Everyone in this trial came through that door.

Scene 8 · The package

What EXCITE cannot separate

Two more limits. The control was usual and customary care, unstandardised across seven sites, so the trial shows the package beating ordinary practice, not how it would fare against an equally intensive rival. And the package is a package: mitt, practice, and the attention of a therapy team arrived together, so no scene here can credit the mitt alone. What was randomised was the whole prescription.

Scene 9 · The verdict

The verdict

EXCITE's answer: force the issue, and a hand that can already open can relearn its job, in the laboratory and at home. The mitt was never the therapy. The asking was.

The good hand got a two week holiday. The weak hand got its job back, and kept it for a year. That is the whole finding, and it is enough: for the stroke survivor whose hand can already open a little, the road back runs through being made to use it.

The Mirror Room

Train one arm. The other one gets stronger. One room, two sides, and the gauge on the resting side is the story.

One arm is in a cast. The other arm goes to the gym. That sounds like loyalty to the wrong limb, until the cast comes off and the arm that did nothing has kept its strength, while the arm in the identical cast, on the person who rested both sides, has lost more than a fifth of it.

The phenomenon has a name, cross-education, and it has been on the record since 1894: train one limb, and the same muscle on the other side gets stronger too, without moving, without growing. The strength arrives through the wiring, not the muscle. This page trains one side of a room and watches the other side's gauge.

The cast studies ask the harder question: not whether the resting side can gain, but whether it can refuse to lose. Sixteen healthy students wore a cast on one forearm for four weeks. Half trained the free wrist hard, lowering weights slowly, three times a week. Half did nothing.

When the casts came off, the trained group's casted wrist flexors had lost 2.4 percent of their strength. The resting group's had lost 21.6 percent. The casted muscle even held its size on the scan, and an earlier study with thirty volunteers in casts for three weeks found the same protection for strength. Station 3 · The Wiring

Two hypotheses share one diagram

Hypothesis one · Cross-activation

The pathways themselves retune

Training one side chronically changes the pathways that project to the other side, so the resting muscle becomes easier for the brain to switch on and drive hard.

Hypothesis two · Bilateral access

The programme is stored once, used twice

Training writes a motor programme for the trained side. The untrained side can open the same programme when it is asked for a maximal effort, the way a learned skill transfers.

Nobody knows the mechanism, and the field's own review says so in plain words. Two hypotheses share the wiring diagram. In the first, training one side retunes the pathways that project to the other side, so the resting muscle becomes easier to switch on. In the second, the training is stored as a motor programme, and the other side can borrow the programme when it is asked to push.

The physiology leans neural. When Green and Gabriel measured the untrained limb, its force had risen while the muscle's own electrical signals had not changed at all; what rose was central activation, the drive arriving from above. The muscle did not change. The instructions reaching it did.

Station 5 · The Clinic

Where the mirror earns its keep

In the clinic the cast is not a volunteer's; it is a fracture. Women over fifty with a broken wrist trained the unbroken hand at home from the day of the fracture. Twelve weeks later, the fractured hand gripped 17.3 kilograms against 11.8 in the group that rested, and it bent further. By twenty six weeks the groups had met again: an earlier finish line, not a higher one.

After stroke the same trade is offered to the arm that barely moves: train the stronger side and let the weaker one borrow the drive. Five small controlled studies, 131 people in all, point the same way, toward better strength and function in the more affected arm. The review that pooled them graded the evidence low, with some risk of bias in every study. Promising is the honest word, not proven.

The dynamometer is drawn. The grip values are measured: Magnus et al., Archives of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation, 2013; the stroke pooling is Smyth et al., Physiotherapy, 2023.

Station 6 · The Audit

The claim, held to its size

Hold the claim to its size. The transfer is real, and it is the smaller half of the story: the trained arm in the cast study gained 30.8 percent while its resting twin was only defended. Cross-education is not growth, not a replacement for training the limb itself, and part of what looks like transfer may be learning how to be tested, or tiny mirror contractions under the cast. What it is, is a month not wasted.

The Switchboard

One face, two control rooms, three switches. Flip each lesion and watch which parts of the face go dark.

A stroke takes half a face and leaves the forehead alone. The patient raises both eyebrows, wrinkles the forehead, and cannot move one side of the mouth. Students memorize it as a fact to recite. It is not a fact; it is a wiring diagram, and diagrams are easier to keep than lists.

One picture explains it. The face runs from a small relay in the pons, two halves under different management. The forehead and eyelid half takes orders from both sides of the brain. The mouth half takes orders from one side only, the opposite one. Flip the switches below and watch what goes dark.

Station 1 · The Wiring

One relay, two halves, different management

One manager, across the midline

The last switch is the cable itself. Past the relay, every fibre travels in one nerve; a lesion anywhere along it silences brow, eyelid, and mouth together. No second manager can cover for it. Both hemispheres handed their orders to the relay upstream; downstream, there is only the wire.

The Descent

The arms point at the lesion: why one coma folds its arms and another straightens them, and the one relay that decides.

By a PM&R resident physician

Two postures are taught as a pair to memorise: decorticate, decerebrate, arms folded, arms straight, a number bolted to each. Learned that way they are trivia. Watched at the bedside they are something else: a patient who cannot follow one command is still answering a question, and his arms are doing the answering.

The answer is a level. Partway down the brainstem sits one small relay whose textbook job is to bend the arms. Damage above it leaves the relay connected, and the arms fold. Damage below it cuts the relay off, and the arms straighten. The posture is not random damage. It is a readout: which circuits are still wired in.

Level 1 · The Exam

Six rungs, and the arms do the talking

The motor scale of the Glasgow Coma Scale, drawn as the descent's first ladder. The two highlighted rungs are the postures.

The scale that records it is older than most of the doctors using it. In 1974, Teasdale and Jennett published the Glasgow Coma Scale, whose motor ladder runs from obeying commands at six down to no response at one. Posturing lives at the bottom of that ladder, where pain is answered by the arms alone.

Two rungs carry the names. A brisk bend at the elbow, the arm pulling away, scores four: normal flexion. A slower bend whose features are clearly predominantly abnormal scores three: decorticate. Straightening at the elbow scores two: extension, decerebrate. When the two sides disagree, the scale records the better answer, a rule that admits how untidy real posturing can be.

Level 2 · The Relay

One relay in the midbrain

The textbook wiring, drawn as a model. It is a teaching picture, and Level 6 below holds it to its size.

The picture that makes the pair memorable is wiring. The main line for voluntary movement, the corticospinal tract, runs from cortex to cord. Partway down, in the midbrain, sits the red nucleus, a relay whose tract, the rubrospinal, reaches the upper cord and favours the muscles that bend the arm. Lower down, older systems favour straightening muscles.

Notice what the relay does not serve: the legs. Its tract fades out in the upper cord, at the levels that run the arms, while the legs' straightening drive rises from lower down and survives either injury. So the legs point the same way in both postures, straight, and only the arms get a vote.

Two Shocks

Two conditions share the word shock after a spinal cord injury. One drops the blood pressure. The other switches off the reflexes. They are not the same problem, and they do not run on the same clock.

A spinal cord injury can leave a patient with two conditions that share one word, and students have been merging them into a single blur ever since. They are opposites. One is a plumbing failure: the blood pressure falls within hours. The other is an electrical silence: the reflexes switch off, then return over weeks. Same injury, two systems, two clocks.

The names are the trap. Neurogenic shock is the plumbing problem: blood pressure and pulse. Spinal shock is the wiring problem, a temporary shutdown of reflexes and function below the injury. A review states the split in one sentence: one term describes the haemodynamic changes, the other a reversible loss of reflex function. This page keeps them apart.

One house, two systems, two clocks. The plumbing fails in hours; the wiring fails for weeks. Watch the clocks at the top.

The vagus nerve, the brake on the heart, travels outside the cord entirely. One injury cuts the squeeze and the accelerator, and leaves the brake connected.

The trunk line is a drawn metaphor. The anatomy is sourced: sympathetic outflow from the first thoracic to the second lumbar segments, Henke, Billington and Gater, Journal of Personalized Medicine, 2022; the two terms split, Mack, The Open Pediatric Medicine Journal, 2013.

Room 2 · The Pipes

Pressure falls, and the pulse will not hurry

The vessels

The squeeze switches off above T6

The great vascular bed of the abdomen takes its sympathetic orders through segments a high injury disconnects, which is why the level line is drawn at the sixth thoracic segment.

The heart

The accelerator is cut, the brake is not

Sympathetic drive reaches the heart from the upper thoracic cord. The vagus nerve arrives from outside the cord, so its slowing signal survives the injury intact.

Neurogenic shock needs a high cut. The loss of sympathetic tone happens with injuries above the sixth thoracic segment, because the abdomen's great vascular bed takes its orders from there. Below the cut the vessels relax and stay relaxed. Blood pools in a container too wide for it. The pressure falls not because fluid left the body, but because the pipes opened.

The pulse is the tell. In bleeding, a falling pressure comes with a racing heart. Here the heart has lost its accelerator, the sympathetic fibres from the upper thoracic cord, while its brake answers as loudly as ever. So the pressure falls and the pulse stays slow, the paradox that gives it away. The failure can persist for one to six weeks.

The vessels and gauges are drawn. The level line and the duration are sourced: loss of sympathetic tone with injuries above the sixth thoracic segment, and persistence of one to six weeks, Mack, The Open Pediatric Medicine Journal, 2013.

How common is it? That depends on the ruler; the literature says so itself. Now cross the hall. Spinal shock is the silence below the cut: movement, sensation and reflexes switched off, limbs floppy, bladder quiet. In the four phase model the field teaches, day one is areflexia: the cord below has lost the descending signals that kept it primed. Over the next two days, reflexes flicker back, as starved receptors grow supersensitive to any signal that remains.

The first light is odd: the bulbocavernosus reflex, tested at the bedside, returns early, and many clinicians call its return the end of spinal shock. The order matters more than the date. Skin reflexes, the polysynaptic ones, return before tendon jerks: bulbocavernosus, cremasteric, ankle jerk, Babinski response, knee jerk. Others end it only when tendon reflexes recover, weeks later. The paper lists both endings.

Room 6 · The Fine Print

What the picture cannot hold

The repair target has moved once. In 2013, neurosurgical guidelines suggested a mean arterial pressure of 85 to 90 for seven days, their lowest grade. The 2024 update softened it into a band, a floor of 75 to 80 and a ceiling of 90 to 95, for three to seven days, a weak suggestion on low quality evidence. This page teaches the picture, not the protocol.

Hold the whole picture loosely. Spinal shock is a contested label; its four phases are a model built partly from basic neuroscience, not a stopwatch. The other shock's textbook portrait, pressure down, pulse slow, is real but rarer than the portrait. What survives is the split: if the pressure is falling, think pipes. If the reflexes are missing, think wiring. Then check which clock you are on.

Recap

So what have we learned

Removing a lung does not halve the engine. At six months, group volumes sat a third down (36, FEV1 34, 33 percent), uptake a fifth down, breathlessness the most reported stop reason. Speech: mild to moderate dysarthria, speech hard to produce clearly, though language understanding and expression stayed intact. Tone mildly increased; no focal paresis was reported; sensation normal. The authors reported mild cognitive impairment and moderate motor deficits, less severe than expected.

Scene five · The contrast

Born without it is not the same as losing it

Acquired cerebellar disease can affect planning, spatial skills, emotion, and language. This cohort does not compare congenital with adult loss, or measure adult recovery.

Kept separate from her case: Schmahmann and Sherman (1998) studied 20 patients with disease confined to the cerebellum. Worst from posterior lobe and vermis lesions, it disturbed executive function, spatial cognition, personality, and language; anterior lesions changed little.

The PM&R Files · Essay 35 · The second what if

What If You Stayed in Bed for Three Weeks?

In 1966, five healthy 20 year olds in Dallas volunteered to do nothing: three weeks of complete bed rest, no weight bearing, the way heart attacks were treated at the time. It cost them more than being alive did. Their maximal oxygen uptake fell 27 percent in twenty days. Thirty years of aging, measured in the same five bodies, took only about 12 percent. Then the story turns: at about age 50, six months of training handed the whole thirty years back. This essay races the two clocks on one gauge, then asks what the race means for a hospital ward that still prescribes the bed.

By a PM&R resident physician

The rule of the what if series is simple. The counterfactual is the hook, never the evidence. Every measured number below carries its source, the tank's path between endpoints is drawn rather than measured and labeled as such, and nothing here is a prediction for any one patient. What makes this case special is that the experiment was actually run, and then life itself ran the control arm for thirty years.

Scene one · The ward, 1966

Five beds, one experiment

Dallas, summer 1966. Five healthy college aged volunteers, all about 20, checked into a research ward and went to bed, completely: no standing, no weight bearing, not for a shower or a stroll, for twenty days. Step 1 of 4 · Baseline, 1966

Both clocks at zero. The tank is full: 3.3 liters per minute of maximal oxygen uptake, in five men who were healthy, young, and not athletes. This is the level every later number on the chart is judged against.

Step 2 of 4 · The bed clock

Twenty days, no weight bearing. The tank falls to 2.4, a 27 percent loss, and the fall runs through the pump: stroke volume down from 104 to 74 milliliters at maximal work. Three weeks of stillness did this, and the red line it leaves behind is the mark to beat.

Step 3 of 4 · The life clock

Now the control arm nobody designed. The men trained back in 1966, then simply lived: jobs, families, birthdays. Thirty years later their tank read 2.9. Aging, given three decades and a head start from a refilled tank, never reached the floor the bed found in twenty days.

Step 4 of 4 · The reversal

The second training block, at about age 50, runs six months instead of eight weeks. The tank returns to exactly 3.3. The thirty years are handed back in full, and the age 20 trained peak of 3.9 stays out of reach. The essay keeps that limit on the page.

Step 1 of 3 · The heart, first and fastest

Bed rest's first bill arrives at the pump. Saltin's group concluded that the lost capacity ran through cardiac output, and a 2015 reanalysis of their own data agreed on the direction. Less stroke volume, a lower ceiling, and a higher price for every ordinary task.

Step 2 of 3 · Muscle and sugar, within a week

Modern studies catch the next systems sooner than anyone assumed. A single week costs measurable muscle and nearly a third of insulin sensitivity, in volunteers as young and healthy as the Dallas five were.

Step 3 of 3 · Bone, the long receipt

Bone moves on a slower clock and keeps the longest ledger. Seventeen weeks unloaded the heel by a tenth, and the hip had not recovered half a year after the men stood up again. What the bed takes in weeks, the skeleton repays in seasons, if at all.
